\begin{abstract}
Medical visual question answering (VQA) over radiology images combines perception, question interpretation,
answer synthesis and verification---steps that fail in different ways and are hidden inside a single model call.
We present MedVisionQuery, an agentic system in which a Haystack Agent controller decides, case by case, how to
look at the image, answer, verify the answer \emph{against the image}, and whether to re-examine a specific region,
revise, abstain or finish. Perception is question-aware, the answerer and the verifier both see the image, and a
different, higher-tier model verifies. We evaluate on \NCases{} questions sampled at random from the whole VQA-RAD
dataset against a single-prompt baseline, a hand-designed workflow and a fixed-pipeline ablation, all on the same
answering model, scoring with a strict rule-based scorer and a blinded LLM judge. The single prompt is more accurate
than the agentic loop (\BaseStrict\% vs.\ \AgStrict\% strict, \BaseJudged\% vs.\ \AgJudged\% judged; McNemar
$p=\PairBaseP$ and $p=\PairBaseJP$, significant only under the judge), the loop and the fixed pipeline do not differ significantly ($p=\PairPlP$), and
the loop's higher-tier verifier answering alone beats it (\BlindStrict\%, $p=\BlindAgP$). On the questions it answers, the
loop matches the single prompt (\AnsweredAgAcc\% vs.\ \AnsweredBaseAcc\%); its \PathAbstainN{} abstentions account for
the gap. Its value is calibrated confidence: answers its check verifies are \SigVerAcc\% correct, its
other answers \UnverAcc\%---at about \CostRatio$\times$ the cost of a single prompt. Code is available at
\url{https://github.com/clarence-ck/med-vqa-rad-agentic}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Definition and Motivation}
% ============================================================================
Medical VQA is a fundamentally multi-stage reasoning problem~\cite{lin2023survey}. As established in the literature,
a complete medical VQA system must address at minimum an image encoder, a question encoder, a multimodal fusion
component, and an answer prediction module~\cite{lin2023survey}---but these four components conceal distinct
reasoning sub-problems that interact in clinically important ways. Given a radiology image and a clinical question,
a system must separately solve: (1)~\emph{perception}---what is visible in the image that bears on the question;
(2)~\emph{question interpretation}---what type of reasoning and answer is required, since different question types
demand fundamentally different skills~\cite{liu2023q2a,liu2023conditional}; (3)~\emph{synthesis}---how to integrate
visual evidence into a grounded answer; (4)~\emph{verification}---whether the answer is actually supported by the
visual evidence, a stage explicitly modelled in recent work as a ``Confirm'' module for cross-modal
validation~\cite{syed2025medvctp}; and (5)~\emph{control}---deciding, from what verification finds, whether to
re-examine the image, revise the answer, abstain or stop.

These sub-tasks interact in non-trivial ways, which motivates making them explicit steps: what to look at depends
on the question; the verifier must check cross-modal consistency between the image and the generated answer; and
clinical safety requires explicit guardrails, abstention and disclaimers when confidence is low.

\paragraph{Existing work: classification approaches.} Early and still-prevalent medical VQA methods frame the task as
multi-class classification over a fixed answer vocabulary~\cite{lin2023survey}. The VQA-Med
challenge~\cite{benabacha2019vqamed} established the dominant paradigm: an image encoder and a question encoder
produce fused features passed to a classification head. MEVF~\cite{nguyen2019mevf} extended this with meta-learning
(MAML) and a denoising autoencoder (CDAE), feeding image features into a bilinear attention network (BAN) followed by
a classifier. PMC-VQA~\cite{zhang2023pmcvqa} proposed MedVInT-TE, similarly mapping image--question pairs to answers
via an encoder and classification head. Classification approaches are computationally efficient on close-ended
questions but constrained to answers seen during training and perform poorly on open-ended questions requiring
free-form responses.

\paragraph{Existing work: generation approaches.} To handle open-ended questions, a parallel line of work adopts
autoregressive generation. Bazi et al.~\cite{bazi2023} proposed a full transformer encoder--decoder architecture
demonstrating gains on VQA-RAD and PathVQA. PMC-VQA's MedVInT-TD~\cite{zhang2023pmcvqa} further demonstrated that an
LLM decoder can match or exceed classification-based performance when fine-tuned on large biomedical corpora.
LLaVA-Med~\cite{li2023llavamed}, accepted at NeurIPS 2023, adapted the general-domain LLaVA architecture to
biomedicine via curriculum learning on PMC-15M figure--caption pairs and GPT-4-generated instruction-following data.
RadFM~\cite{wu2025radfm} scaled this into a generalist radiology foundation model capable of VQA, report generation,
and rationale diagnosis. R-LLaVA~\cite{chen2025rllava} extended LLaVA-Med by injecting clinician-annotated regions of
interest into the CLIP encoder, improving spatial and contextual comprehension.

\paragraph{Existing work: hybrid approaches.} Q2ATransformer~\cite{liu2023q2a} proposed a unified transformer
framework introducing learnable candidate answer embeddings as a decoder, querying the fused image--question
representation for each answer class. Although framed as classification, candidate answer embeddings interact through
transformer attention, incorporating aspects of generative reasoning. This hybrid achieves state-of-the-art
performance on VQA-RAD and PathVQA.

\paragraph{The gap that remains.} These approaches---whether
classification~\cite{benabacha2019vqamed,nguyen2019mevf,zhang2023pmcvqa}, generation~\cite{bazi2023,li2023llavamed,wu2025radfm,chen2025rllava},
or hybrid~\cite{liu2023q2a}---answer in a single end-to-end model invocation, which makes it hard to localise
failures, adapt the reasoning to what the image shows, or enforce clinical safety constraints. Recent work adds
explicit structure: MedVCTP~\cite{syed2025medvctp} is a training-free See--Think--Confirm pipeline whose last stage
checks cross-modal consistency, and Qiu et al.~\cite{qiu2025chain} ground answers in a chain of evidence; MedVCTP's
stages, however, run in a fixed order. MedVisionQuery instead uses an agent controller that coordinates question-aware perception,
answering and image-grounded verification, deciding per case whether to re-examine the image, revise, abstain or
finish, with structured failure handling.

We target the \textbf{VQA-RAD} benchmark~\cite{lau2018vqarad}, a clinically generated radiology VQA dataset; its
Hugging Face release contains 2,244 image--question pairs, each with a reference answer, across close-ended and
open-ended question types. Because no system is trained, we sample the evaluation questions from the whole dataset
rather than only its test split (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Agent Architecture}
% ============================================================================
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{system_diagram.png}
  \caption{MedVisionQuery. One Haystack Agent (the controller) calls five tools in a loop, reading each result
  before choosing the next: question analysis, question-aware perception (\texttt{look}), answer synthesis,
  image-grounded verification (\texttt{check}) and \texttt{finish}. The tools are not agents; \texttt{check} only
  recommends a re-look, a revised answer, abstention or acceptance, and the controller decides. A decision-and-safety stage keeps the
  best-verified answer, measures self-consistency and applies guardrails.}
  \label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Controller loop.} A fresh Haystack \texttt{Agent} is created per request (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). Its
five tools are bound to per-request working memory holding the image, the question analysis, and every look,
answer and check; the controller sees only short text results and chooses the next call. Unlike the
ablation, whose only branch is one conditional re-run, the controller chooses the step after every check: on the \NCases{} evaluation cases the controller
finished directly in \PathDirectN{} cases, re-examined a region in \PathRelookN, revised its answer in
\PathReanswerN{} and abstained in \PathAbstainN. Budgets
cap the loop at two focused re-looks, three answers and 16 steps, with a safety net that answers or checks before
finishing if the controller stalls.

\paragraph{Roles.} The \emph{controller} is the planner: it chooses every next step from the results so far.
The per-request \emph{working memory} (image, question analysis, looks, answers, checks) is the memory; the tools
read from and write to it.
\textbf{\texttt{analyze\_question}} (task understanding) classifies the question type and expected answer format.
\textbf{\texttt{look(focus)}} (perception) examines the image \emph{with the question in mind}; with a focus, it
zooms: the named region is located, cropped and enlarged, and examined beside the full image (a plain re-look if
it cannot be located); the first look rejects
non-radiology images.
\textbf{\texttt{answer(guidance)}} (executor) answers from the image plus all observations, applying the
verifier's guidance on revisions.
\textbf{\texttt{check}} (evaluator, a higher-tier model) has two parts. A \emph{blind reading}, made once per episode: the
verifier answers from the image and question alone, without the proposed answer or the looker's notes. A
\emph{review}: it checks the answer against the image (grounding, contradictions, format) and returns a status
(verified, partially verified, low confidence, unsupported), the issues found and a next step---\emph{accept},
\emph{relook} with a focus, \emph{reanswer} with guidance, or \emph{abstain}; the guidance may include a suggested
corrected answer. An answer is verified only if the review passes and the blind reading agrees (by word rules, or for
open-ended answers by meaning, judged by the verifier); the controller learns only whether they agree.
\textbf{\texttt{finish(abstain)}} ends the episode; a normal finish is refused while the latest answer is unchecked.

\paragraph{Model and tool choices.} All roles use OpenAI models, sized by need and configurable per role. The
controller and question analysis make simple decisions (\texttt{gpt-6-luna}, no/low reasoning effort); perception
and answering use \texttt{gpt-6-luna} with medium effort, and the answerer always sees the image, because a
text description loses the visual detail many questions depend on. Verification uses a different, higher-tier model
(\texttt{gpt-6.1-sol}, 20$\times$ the per-token price) so that the answering model never verifies itself; since its guidance can suggest answers,
we also score it answering alone as a reference. Haystack's \texttt{Agent}/\texttt{Tool}
abstraction provides the tool-calling loop and exit conditions; keeping the image in per-request memory rather than
in the controller's context keeps the controller cheap.

\paragraph{Baselines and ablation.} The \emph{single-shot baseline} sends the image and question in one prompt. The
\emph{workflow baseline} is a fixed chain---a question-agnostic image description, question analysis, then
hand-written rules per question type. The \emph{fixed-pipeline ablation} is a controller instructed to call six tools in a prescribed
order---\texttt{interpret\_image}, \texttt{analyze\_question}, \texttt{filter\_image\_context},
\texttt{route\_difficulty}, \texttt{reason\_answer}, \texttt{verify\_answer}---re-running the sequence once when
verification flags a fixable or high-severity problem. It uses the same models but a question-agnostic image description, no zoom, no
blind check and a text-only verifier, so comparing against it measures the agentic design as a whole, not control
alone. A demo UI (Section~\ref{sec:ui}) exposes all systems.

% ============================================================================
\section{Multimodal Grounding}
% ============================================================================
The system uses two modalities, the radiology image and the text question, and they interact at every step that
determines the answer: the question (and any focus) steers what \texttt{look} examines; \texttt{answer} and
\texttt{check} receive the image directly alongside the text observations, because a text description of the image
loses the visual detail they depend on; only question analysis and the controller work from text. The evaluation's
LLM judge compares answers with the reference as text. Because the verifier sees the image, its
``explanation grounded in evidence'' and ``no contradictions'' checks are genuine cross-modal agreement checks.

% ============================================================================
\section{Control and Failure Handling}
% ============================================================================
\textbf{Detection.} Image-grounded verification (five checks and a status), seven failure categories
(\emph{unsupported claim}, \emph{low confidence}, \emph{visual contradiction}, \emph{partially verified},
\emph{question--answer mismatch}, \emph{generation unstable}, \emph{parser failure}) and an eighth,
\emph{self-inconsistent}, from resampling.
\textbf{Recovery.} Instead of re-running the whole pipeline, the controller zooms into the region the verifier
names or revises the answer with the verifier's guidance. When the blind reading disagrees, the controller is told
only \emph{that} it disagrees, never the blind answer (the review's guidance may still suggest one). A later answer replaces an earlier one only if its check
is strictly better, because focused re-looks can over-read.
\textbf{Self-critique.} After finishing, the answerer is re-asked independently twice; low agreement adds a failure
category and flags the case for review.
\textbf{Fallback.} The controller may abstain (reported as an abstention, never coerced into ``no''); a guardrail
adds a disclaimer and a review flag for low-confidence, unsupported, high-severity or self-inconsistent cases;
non-radiology images are rejected, and the loop stops rather than answer blind if the first look fails.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
% ============================================================================
\paragraph{Setup.} \NCases{} questions drawn at random from the whole VQA-RAD dataset, keeping its close/open-ended
mix (seed 51511; \NClose{} close-ended, \NOpen{} open-ended; one known-bad item and an earlier evaluation's 300
questions excluded). 277 carry the train label, so scores are not directly comparable with published test-split
results. All four
systems run on the same cases with the same answering model. \emph{Strict scoring}: yes/no answers must start with
the correct word; binary terms must name the correct term and not its opposite; counts must match; open-ended answers
must contain every content word of the reference (aliases and plurals folded; $\geq$60\% for references longer than
three words), with left/right conflicts and denials counted wrong and embedding similarity~\cite{deka2022spubmed}
only as a strict fallback. Because word matching both rejects correct paraphrases and accepts vague answers that
contain the reference word, a \emph{blinded LLM judge} (\texttt{gpt-6-astra}, a model that neither answers nor
verifies in any system; answers shuffled and anonymised) scores every open-ended answer as a second, independent
measure; a 30-verdict spot check agreed with 29 credit decisions (in the other, it was too harsh on an agentic answer). Paired comparisons use the exact McNemar test; 95\% intervals are about
$\pm$\CIHalf{} points. The baseline and agentic loop were re-run on \VarCases{} cases to measure stability. API cost of the
evaluation: \$\CostTotal{} (\$\CostRun{} runs, \$\CostRepeats{} repeats, \$\CostJudge{} judge). Wall-clock time:
\MinEval\,min for the main run (four cases at a time), \MinRepeats\,min for the sequential repeats and
\MinJudge\,min for the judge.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:main}. The single prompt is ahead of the agentic loop (\PairBaseB{} vs.\
\PairBaseA{} discordant cases, $p=\PairBaseP$; judged \PairBaseJB{} vs.\ \PairBaseJA, $p=\PairBaseJP$); the loop and

loop ($p=\BlindAgP$). The gap to the single prompt comes from abstention: on the \AnsweredN{} questions it answered,
the loop scores \AnsweredAgAcc\% versus \AnsweredBaseAcc\%, while on its \PathAbstainN{} abstentions the single prompt
was right in \AbstainBaseRight{} and the loop's own last answer in \AbstainOwnRight. The loop's mechanisms work
individually: revision fixed \RevFixed{} wrong first answers and broke \RevBroken{} (\RevN{} revised episodes), and when
the blind check objected to a first answer it was wrong in \BlindFlagWrong{} of \BlindFlagN{} cases. They do not lift
the hardest cases above the single prompt: answered questions with a zoomed look score \ZoomAg\% vs.\ \ZoomBase\%
and re-answered cases \PathReanswerAg\% vs.\ \PathReanswerBase\% (Table~\ref{tab:paths}). A post-hoc check (rule
chosen after seeing these results, not a system result) locates the loss: taking the blind reading whenever the loop
abstained or disagreed with it would have raised the loop to \PostHoc\% strict, close to the verifier's own
\BlindStrict\%.

\paragraph{Failure case analysis.}
\emph{Self-consistency:} two independent re-answers per case; high consistency means \ScHighAcc\% accuracy,
medium/low \ScMedLowRange\%, so low consistency marks uncertainty rather than certain error.
\emph{Cross-modal agreement:} the verifier checks the answer against the image, and its status is predictive:
verified answers are \SigVerAcc\% correct, other answers \UnverAcc\%.
\emph{Verifier model:} a higher-tier model verifies the answering model; after its checks, \NotDirect{} of \NCases{}
episodes leave the direct path.
\emph{Disagreement:} the three systems disagree on \DisagreePct\% of cases; when exactly one system is right, it is the
agentic loop \WinsAg{} times, the single prompt \WinsBase{} and the workflow \WinsWf.
\emph{Stability:} on \VarCases{} repeated cases, \VarBaseFlip\% of single-prompt answers flipped between right and
wrong, versus \VarAgFlip\% for the agentic loop.
Five patterns dominate the errors: (i) a shared perception blind spot: under the judge, 65 questions (22\%), mostly open-ended
ones asking for a specific location, finding or diagnosis, defeat the single prompt, the loop and the pipeline alike;
no check catches a misreading that looker and verifier share, and 51 of the 59 the loop answered still get high
self-consistency; (ii) abstention withholding a right answer: the verifier's review recommended 21 of the
25 abstentions, 14 times although its own blind reading agreed with the loop's best-verified answer; (iii) the hardest questions resisting re-looks, with an occasional revision breaking a
right answer; (iv) benchmark quirks---ungrammatical questions, or references needing detail an image rarely
settles---where abstaining or hedging is scored wrong; and (v) workflow rigidity, where fixed rules answer
``Abdomen'' or ``Chest'' when a specific finding or side is needed.

\section{Reflection}
% ============================================================================
\paragraph{When agentic behaviour is beneficial.} On this benchmark, not for accuracy: the single prompt is more
accurate (significantly under the judge) and the loop trails its own verifier answering alone. Its checking finds
errors---revision fixes more answers than it breaks, and the blind check's objections are usually right---but its
decisions waste that signal: it abstains, or keeps its own answer, where the verifier's blind reading is often right,
and a loop repaired by deferring to that reading would be little more than its verifier. Its answers are also less
stable between runs. Its benefit is \emph{calibrated confidence}: the verified label separates answers that are
\SigVerAcc\% correct from its other answers at \UnverAcc\%, and every decision is auditable, which suits review workflows that
can act on that signal. Answering with a low-confidence flag instead of abstaining, and letting a disagreeing blind
reading replace a low-confidence answer, tested on fresh questions, are the natural next steps.

\paragraph{Limitations.} About \CostRatio$\times$ the cost and \LatRatio$\times$ the median latency of a single prompt,
for lower accuracy on this benchmark. Abstentions are scored as wrong (one is judged correct, its reference being ``maybe''), so the
abstention policy lowers accuracy by design.
The verifier shares the perception weaknesses of vision models, so agreement is not correctness, and because its
guidance may suggest answers, the loop's contribution cannot be fully separated from the verifier's (a critique-only
verifier would isolate it). \NCases{} questions
give $\pm$\CIHalf-point intervals and noisy per-type estimates; the LLM judge is itself a model, spot-checked but not
human-annotated. The system uses no retrieval, so it cannot draw on knowledge that is not visible in the image.
